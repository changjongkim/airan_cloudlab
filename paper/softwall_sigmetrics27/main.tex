\keywords{AI-RAN, GPU scheduling, neural receiver, conditional recovery, MPS, admission control}

\begin{abstract}
As AI-RAN platforms run radio processing and AI inference on the same GPUs, neural receivers create
GPU work that schedulers cannot see. Until its neural receiver (\NRx) finishes, an uplink transport
block (TB) may still need a second decoding by the conventional receiver. This \emph{pending recovery}
is in no GPU queue, but it becomes mandatory if the receiver fails. This paper presents \sys, a GPU
scheduler that tracks pending recoveries explicitly. \sys gives GPU time to AI only when an independent
verifier confirms that every TB could still be recovered even if every pending receiver failed. It then
keeps each decision valid on the GPU: it checks a latest start time before each launch, applies all
receiver results at once, and frees GPU time only after the GPU reports completion. On two four-GPU
A100 nodes under a validated test timeline, \sys has 0 safety violations in 120 boundary tests, while
admitting AI into idle GPU time fails 40/40. Across 13,716 feasible settings of a capacity model, \sys
admits more AI than static reservation in 26.2\% of them, and its p99 decision time is 1.494~ms with 64
pending recoveries.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Radio access networks (RANs) increasingly run baseband processing on GPUs, and AI-RAN platforms run
radio physical-layer (PHY) processing and AI inference on the same GPUs
~\cite{aerial,yinyangran,interplay,distributedairan}. To use the GPU time that radio processing leaves
idle, these platforms add AI services, such as large language model (LLM) inference, next to
deadline-critical PHY tasks~\cite{caora,haf}. This sharing works only if every radio task still
finishes before its deadline~\cite{concordia}, and ordinary GPU sharing can disrupt 5G
processing~\cite{yinyangran}. Neural receivers make this harder. A neural receiver replaces channel
estimation, equalization, and demapping with a learned model, and whether it decodes a block is known
only after it runs~\cite{deeprx,nrx5g}. However, the GPU schedulers of these platforms protect only the
radio work that is already in a GPU queue or in a resource reservation.

The difficulty comes from how neural reception falls back. A neural receiver (\NRx) is optional work:
if it succeeds in time, it delivers the uplink transport block (TB), and if it fails, is late, or loses
its result, the conventional receiver must decode the same TB. Until the \NRx result is known, this
recovery is in no GPU queue, yet it must still be able to finish before the radio deadline. When several
cells run \NRx together, their recoveries compete for one conventional decoder, and their failures can
happen together. In addition, NVIDIA Multi-Process Service (MPS) caps the GPU threads of each process,
but it does not bound host scheduling, queueing, or process restarts~\cite{nvidiampS}. Thus, a scheduler
that fills idle GPU time with AI can push a future recovery past its deadline, and a scheduler that
reserves every possible recovery wastes the GPU time that a successful \NRx frees.

Existing systems share accelerators between radio processing and other workloads in different ways. As
Table~\ref{tab:closest} summarizes, they differ in what their schedulers protect or allocate. vRAN and
AI-RAN resource managers~\cite{yinyangran,cloudric,concordia,interplay,caora,haf,distributedairan}
allocate compute shares, queued requests, reserved capacity, or worker placement between RAN functions
and other workloads. GPU scheduling systems~\cite{daris,reef,xsched,nvtaskset} partition, prioritize, or
preempt GPU stages, kernels, and contexts. PHY and edge platforms~\cite{arches,ocudu,smec} choose PHY
models, expose timing classes inside the distributed unit (DU), or coordinate edge service-level
objectives (SLOs). However, all of them schedule resources or queued jobs. Their public designs neither
represent the recovery that a neural receiver may need nor keep an admission decision valid until the
GPU finishes, and a decision that is correct when made can become wrong before it runs.

In this paper, we present \sys, a recovery-safe GPU scheduler that shares AI-RAN GPUs between neural
receivers and AI inference. \sys records each recovery that may still be needed as a \emph{pending
recovery} and accepts a decision only when an independent verifier confirms that every TB could still be
recovered if all pending receivers failed. Specifically, \sys introduces 1) a scheduling model that
decides together which TBs try \NRx, when recoveries run, and when AI runs, over one set of pending
recoveries shared by several cuPHY instances, 2) enforcement that keeps each accepted decision valid on
the GPU by checking a latest start time before each launch, applying all receiver results at once, and
freeing GPU time only after the GPU reports completion, and 3) a prediction, based only on validated time
bounds, that classifies each state as \QSU, \QSN, \MI, or \UQ before it runs. Our evaluation on two
four-GPU A100 nodes under a validated test timeline shows 0 safety violations in 120 boundary tests for
\sys and 40/40 for idle-time admission. When AI requests arrive in bursts taken from BurstGPT with a
200~ms deadline, \sys serves 6.2-18.5\% more AI tokens on time than recovery-first, its variant without
AI-first order, and static reservation serves none, while idle-time admission misses the recovery
deadline in up to 10.0\% of periods. On one GPU, \sys makes the same decisions, and early AI slots that
share the GPU with \NRx serve 2.6-70.3\% more on-time AI tokens without losing a timely \NRx result. In
1,200 periods on physical GPUs, the shared GPU finishes every period before its scheduled end, and under
seven kinds of injected faults every TB commits exactly once before its result deadline. Across 13,716
feasible settings of a capacity model, \sys admits more AI than static reservation in 3,600 (26.2\%).
The scheduler matches an exact checker on 16,023 states, and its p99 decision time is 1.494~ms with 64
pending recoveries. On the original trace, \sys and recovery-first deliver the same 349,387 timely
tokens.

\section{Background and Motivation}

\subsection{AI-RAN on shared GPUs}

AI-RAN platforms run RAN functions and AI services on the same GPUs
~\cite{aerial,yinyangran,cloudric,interplay,caora,haf}. In our setting, these GPUs run three kinds of
work: optional neural reception, mandatory conventional reception, and external AI.

\noindent\textbf{Neural Reception.}
An uplink TB is decoded either by a conventional receiver, such as the cuPHY physical uplink shared
channel (PUSCH) pipeline~\cite{aerial}, or by a neural receiver~\cite{deeprx,nrx5g}. A cuPHY instance runs
the PUSCH pipelines of several cells, and in our test timeline each cell sends one TB per period
(Figure~\ref{fig:airan-background}). A TB commits the \NRx result if it passes its cyclic redundancy check
(CRC) by the \NRx deadline; otherwise, one conventional recovery must finish before the recovery
deadline. Figure~\ref{fig:debt}a shows that the \NRx result is hard to predict. In a synthetic fading
channel without a direct path (block Rayleigh, 500 TBs per point) and in two 3GPP channels with a direct
path (CDL-D and CDL-E~\cite{tr38901,sionna}, 50 TBs per point), the \NRx success rate rises from near
0\% to near 100\% within 1~dB of the signal quality at which the conventional receiver decodes half of
the TBs. In CDL-D and CDL-E, the conventional receiver also decodes some TBs that \NRx misses. Link
adaptation targets a 10\% block error rate~\cite{ts38214}, which keeps TBs near this transition.

\noindent\textbf{GPU Sharing.}
MPS runs several CUDA processes on one GPU at the same time and caps the share of GPU threads that each
process may use~\cite{nvidiampS}, but it does not bound host scheduling, queueing, process restarts, or
memory allocation. Multi-Instance GPU (MIG) splits a GPU into isolated parts, but a fixed split cannot
reuse the GPU time that a successful \NRx frees. Figure~\ref{fig:execution}a runs Qwen2.5-1.5B
prefill~\cite{qwen25} next to a two-cell, 273-PRB PUSCH pipeline and varies the GPU share given to Qwen.
With one 16-token request, the radio p99 latency stays within 11.9~ms at every share, against
8.9-10.0~ms without AI. With eight 512-token requests, it stays within 13.9~ms up to an 80\% share but
reaches 21.2-22.9~ms at 100\%, where 93 of 1,000 slots miss the 21~ms deadline. Thus, a GPU share is
safe only for the kind of AI work under which it was tested.

\noindent\textbf{Execution Tails.}
Figure~\ref{fig:execution}b compares the slowest runs with the median run. The \NRx TensorRT graph stays
within 3.5\% of its median, but end-to-end paths do not. At the 4.5~ms result deadline of the Aerial
testMAC~\cite{aerial}, cross-GPU receiver pairs take up to 4.0 and 3.7 times their median on a Python
path and a partly native C++/CUDA path, and host scheduling takes up to 29 times its median. Thus, a
schedule computed from GPU kernel times can become wrong before it runs.

Together, these observations tie radio correctness to AI admission decisions that are made before the
receiver result is known. Existing systems split GPU resources between RAN and AI workloads
~\cite{yinyangran,cloudric,interplay,caora,haf} or preempt GPU kernels~\cite{reef,xsched,daris}, but their
public designs do not schedule the recovery that neural reception may need.

\subsection{Challenges in scheduling pending recovery}
\label{sec:requirements}

Existing schedulers admit AI based on the work in GPU queues or on a fixed reservation
~\cite{yinyangran,cloudric,concordia}. With neural reception, this approach faces three challenges: the
number of needed recoveries is uncertain, the cells of several cuPHY instances share one GPU for
recovery, and a decision must stay valid until it runs.

\noindent\textbf{Pending Recovery.}
Before the \NRx result is known, the conventional recovery of a TB may or may not be needed: it is needed
only if the \NRx fails. We call it a \emph{pending recovery}. Figure~\ref{fig:debt}b turns a per-TB failure
probability $p$ into the number of recoveries that a period with four TBs needs; measured periods follow
the curves for independent failures. Admitting AI into idle GPU time reserves no recovery and is wrong
whenever one recovery is needed, which happens in 34\% of periods at $p=0.1$. Static reservation holds
four recoveries, which a period uses only when all four TBs fail, once in $10^4$ periods with
independent failures. A failure correlation of 0.1 or 0.3 within a period makes this all-four event 16 or
116 times more likely. Thus, planning for the typical number of recoveries is unsafe, and holding every
reservation until all results are known wastes the GPU time that successes free.

\noindent\textbf{Scheduling Failures.}
Figure~\ref{fig:scheduling-problem} shows three failures after two timely successes in our test
timeline. Idle-time admission sees an empty queue at the 45~ms \NRx deadline, admits a 256-token AI
request, and pushes a needed recovery past the recovery deadline. Static reservation keeps the
reservations of the two successful TBs and rejects a 128-token AI request that would fit without them.
Schedules checked per cuPHY instance are each correct alone, but they claim the same time on the shared
recovery GPU. Thus, a scheduler must track pending recovery explicitly, replan whenever a result arrives,
and check one schedule for all pending recoveries that share a GPU.

\noindent\textbf{Execution Gaps.}
A schedule that is correct when made must stay correct when it runs. Table~\ref{tab:counterexamples}
lists seven failure cases that define this requirement. In our development runs, a 23.5~ms host stall
launched a 128-token AI request outside its scheduled time, and applying simultaneous results one by one
created an intermediate state that never existed. Thus, the scheduler must count launch overhead in
every schedule, apply simultaneous results at once, and keep GPU time owned until the GPU reports
completion. Section~\ref{sec:design} describes how \sys does this.

\section{Backstop Design}
\label{sec:design}

\sys treats pending recovery as explicit state and keeps each decision valid until the GPU finishes the
work it started. This section presents the architecture, pending-recovery scheduling, enforcement on the
GPU, and the scheduling algorithm, and then states what \sys guarantees and how it is implemented.

\subsection{Overall architecture}
\label{sec:design-overall}

\sys schedules pending recovery and external AI on the GPUs of one AI-RAN node.
Figure~\ref{fig:overall} shows its architecture. A control layer on the host makes the decisions, and
long-running GPU workers under MPS execute them. The figure follows one period through seven steps
(\ding{182}-\ding{188}) in an example in which TB1 and TB2 succeed, TB3 fails its CRC, TB4 is late, and
TB5 is rejected when it arrives.

\noindent\textbf{Control Layer.}
One host coordinator keeps four objects. \textit{Decision pages} record, for each TB, the \NRx result,
the resulting action, and the version of the decision. \textit{Pending recoveries} list every TB whose
conventional recovery may still be needed, with its recovery window; in the example, TB3 and TB4 remain
with the 45-153~ms window, while TB1 and TB2 are cancelled. The \textit{shared-GPU schedule} holds the
current schedule $X_t$ of the shared GPU and a candidate $X'$ that adds one AI slot, with its 5~ms launch
budget, before the recovery deadline. The \textit{independent verifier} commits $X'$ or keeps $X_t$.

\noindent\textbf{Execution Layer.}
Long-running MPS workers execute all GPU work. On the four-GPU node (Figure~\ref{fig:overall}b), GPU0 and
GPU1 run the cuPHY instances that own TB1-TB3 and TB4-TB5 with a 50\% MPS share. GPU3 runs the TensorRT
\NRx as a CUDA graph with an 80\% share, receives IQ samples and channel estimates over NVLink, and
returns log-likelihood ratios (LLRs). GPU2 is the shared GPU: it runs the cuPHY recovery worker with an
80\% share and the Qwen worker with a 20\% share, and it writes the completion markers. On one GPU
(Figure~\ref{fig:overall}a), the same processes share the device and exchange IQ, LLR, and TB buffers
through CUDA IPC.

\noindent\textbf{Scheduling Loop.}
Each cuPHY instance writes the \NRx result of its TBs to a decision page (\ding{182}). At the \NRx
deadline, \sys applies all results at once to the pending-recovery table (\ding{183}). A fast scheduler
proposes a candidate that reschedules the needed recoveries around one AI slot (\ding{184}), and the
independent verifier checks the worst-case recovery schedule, the buffer slots, and the version before it
commits $X'$ or keeps $X_t$ (\ding{185}). The coordinator then sends each action with its version
(\ding{186}) and sends the AI slot with a latest start time to the Qwen worker (\ding{187}). A completion
marker or a ``not launched'' reply frees the slot (\ding{188}), and the shared GPU runs the needed
recoveries in the committed order.

\noindent\textbf{Scheduling Inputs.}
The scheduler uses only information that is visible at the current event. A radio job and an AI job are
\begin{equation}
 J_i=(r_i,d_i,h_i,c_i,K_i,q_i), \qquad
 A_a=(u_a,v_a,\ell_a,b_a,g_a),
\end{equation}
where $r_i$ and $d_i$ are the arrival time and the result deadline, $h_i$ is the owning cuPHY
instance, $c_i$ is the time bound of the conventional recovery, $K_i$ holds the \NRx workers that may
decode it, and $q_i$ is the value of trying \NRx; $u_a$, $v_a$, $\ell_a$, $b_a$, and $g_a$ are the AI
arrival time, value, deadline, time bound, and margin. Decisions cannot use future CRC results, future
arrivals, actual service times, or hidden channel labels.

\noindent\textbf{Validated Modes.}
A time bound can be used for scheduling only under the measurement conditions in which it was validated,
\begin{equation}
 \pi=(\text{GPU},\text{placement},\text{MPS},\text{lifecycle},\text{co-run},\text{software}).
\end{equation}
Therefore, a bound measured on a warm local path does not hold for a cold, restarted, or remote path. The
scheduler marks a state \UQ whenever a needed bound was not measured under the current conditions, even
if a sample finishes in time.

\noindent\textbf{Proposer and Verifier.}
The fast scheduler may use a simple list heuristic and may miss a feasible placement. It cannot create an
unsafe execution because only the verifier can commit a candidate. With this split, the evaluation can
compare decision quality with an exact search while safety does not depend on the heuristic.

\subsection{Pending-recovery scheduling}
\label{sec:design-recovery}

A TB whose \NRx result is unknown may still need a recovery, even though no conventional kernel is
queued. \sys records this work as a pending recovery
\begin{equation}
 R_i=(a_i(t),d_i-g_i,c_i,h_i),
\end{equation}
where $a_i(t)$ is its earliest allowed start. For the set $U(t)$ of TBs without a result, an
\emph{all-fail schedule} gives every $R_i$ its own interval $[s_i,s_i+c_i]$ on its recovery GPU, after
its arrival and before $d_i-g_i$, as if every one of these TBs failed. Data copies and AI slots that use
the same GPU belong to the same schedule.

\noindent\textbf{All-Fail Dominance.}
\sys needs one schedule, not one schedule per combination of results.
\begin{proposition}[All-fail dominance]
If a timely \NRx success only removes its conventional recovery and does not add work to any other job,
a feasible all-fail schedule for $U(t)$ is feasible for every subset $F\subseteq U(t)$ of failed TBs.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Delete the successful TBs from the all-fail schedule. The arrival, deadline, capacity, and non-overlap
constraints of every remaining interval stay the same.
\end{proof}
This result replaces $2^{|U|}$ possible schedules with one executable schedule. It does not apply when a
success creates new mandatory work or changes another time bound; that case needs a new mode.

\noindent\textbf{Admission and Results.}
Figure~\ref{fig:atomic-replan}a shows one period on the shared GPU. When TBs arrive, \sys reserves one
pending recovery per TB before it starts any \NRx (\ding{182}). TB1-TB4 fit before the recovery
deadline, and TB5 is rejected because its reservation would cross the deadline. Therefore, a TB is never
admitted without a conventional path. At the 45~ms \NRx deadline, \sys applies all results at once
(\ding{183}): TB1 and TB2 succeed and cancel their pending recoveries, while TB3 and TB4 fail and keep
theirs. The GPU time that TB1 and TB2 free stays available until the recovery deadline, and the committed
schedule $X_t$ holds two recoveries. Applying the same results one by one is wrong because it can create
intermediate states that never existed together.

\noindent\textbf{Candidate AI Slot.}
Figure~\ref{fig:atomic-replan}b adds one AI slot to $X_t$. For a 128-token request, the candidate $X'$
places the 5~ms launch budget and the AI slot first and moves TB3 and TB4 behind them (\ding{184}). The
last recovery still ends before the recovery deadline, and the verifier commits $X'$ as version $g+1$
(\ding{185}). For a 256-token request, TB4 would end after the recovery deadline; the verifier rejects
$X'$ and keeps $X_t$.

\noindent\textbf{Early AI Slot.}
The shared GPU is idle from the TB arrival until the \NRx deadline, when the first recovery can start.
Therefore, \sys also runs the same check when TBs arrive: an \emph{early AI slot} starts right away, and
the verifier commits it only if the all-fail schedule of every admitted TB, with the slot added, still
ends before the recovery deadline. In our test timeline, four recoveries take 100~ms of the 108~ms
recovery window, and an early slot may end at most 8~ms after the \NRx deadline. This limit admits a
16-128-token request and rejects a 256- or 512-token request. On one GPU, the early slot shares the GPU
with \NRx. If the slot delays an \NRx result past the \NRx deadline, that TB only needs its recovery,
which the all-fail schedule already holds. Thus, an early slot can cost \NRx value but not radio safety.

\noindent\textbf{One Shared State.}
The schedule covers everything that shares a GPU: GPUs that share nothing are scheduled separately,
while cuPHY instances that share a recovery GPU need one common set of pending recoveries. The full
runtime state is
\begin{equation}
 X_t=(S_t,Q_t,T_t,L_t,E_t,C_t,G_t),
\end{equation}
where $S$ is the recovery schedule, $Q$ and $T$ are the buffer slots of the receivers and of the data
copies, $L$ holds the AI slots, $E$ holds the completion events and worker epochs, $C$ is the radio commit
state, and $G$ records which cuPHY instance may launch AI. The verifier commits a private candidate $X'$
only when
\begin{align}
\mathrm{VALID}(X') \equiv {}& \mathrm{ExecutableRecovery}(S') \land
 \mathrm{UniqueCredits}(Q',T') \nonumber\\
 &\land\ \mathrm{TimelyAI}(L',E') \land \mathrm{CurrentVersions}(X').
\end{align}
A rejected candidate leaves $X_t$ unchanged, which rules out partial updates and orphan AI slots.

\noindent\textbf{Safety Invariant.}
The verifier and the enforcement in Section~\ref{sec:design-refinement} keep one invariant at every
event.
\begin{invariant}[Recovery, ownership, and single commit]
For every admitted TB and every allowed combination of pending results, a conventional path that
overlaps no other work finishes before the result deadline. No receiver, copy, AI, or output buffer slot
is reused before the GPU reports that its work finished, and each TB commits at most one result.
\end{invariant}

A correct schedule on the host is not enough unless launches and ownership follow it.

the Qwen worker, and the GPU stream, and Figures~\ref{fig:refinement}b-e show each injected fault on the
same kind of timeline.

\noindent\textbf{Launch Check.}
The coordinator commits $L$ as version $g+1$ (\ding{182}) and sends $\mathrm{run}(L,\text{latest start})$
to the Qwen worker (\ding{183}). The request carries the schedule version, the worker epoch, the buffer
slots, and the latest start time. The worker launches only if the current time is at or before the latest
start time (\ding{184}); it then submits the prefill kernels and writes the launch marker
\texttt{L.launch}. Otherwise, it replies ``not launched'' and runs nothing, and the coordinator frees $L$
at once (Figure~\ref{fig:refinement}b).

\noindent\textbf{Freeing GPU Time.}
When the GPU finishes, the worker writes the completion marker \texttt{L.complete} and replies
(\ding{185}). The coordinator frees $L$ only on a marker that matches its version and moves to version
$g+2$ (\ding{186}). An RPC reply does not prove that the GPU finished. Therefore, slots and buffers stay
owned until the matching GPU completion event, and the end of a data copy does not count as the end of a
receiver or a recovery.

\noindent\textbf{Fault Handling.}
Figures~\ref{fig:refinement}c-e show the other injected faults. When the reply is late or lost after the
GPU finished (c), the coordinator times out, finds \texttt{L.complete}, frees $L$ from the marker, and
stops AI admission. When the channel is lost before the GPU finished (d), only \texttt{L.launch} exists;
the coordinator keeps $L$ owned because its kernels may still run, stops AI admission, and continues radio
recovery. A repeated or old \NRx result leaves the radio state unchanged, and a repeated recovery response
produces no second commit because each TB commits once (e).

\noindent\textbf{Buffer Ownership.}
One AI queue can serve several cuPHY instances, but each instance has at most one pending launch request
at a time, and a commit hands over the right to launch only while the local and common schedules are
current. CUDA IPC and NVLink avoid a copy through host memory and also make ownership explicit: each
buffer slot has one version, one completion state, and one completion event that the scheduler sees.
Therefore, \sys counts data transfer inside the validated service time. Reconnecting the same worker
keeps the version through the markers, while replacing a worker process without a durable log remains
\UQ.

\subsection{Scheduling algorithm}
\label{sec:design-algorithm}

\sys runs one algorithm at every event, and only the verifier can commit its result. Algorithm~1 rejects
optional work whenever it cannot show that the measurement conditions hold, that every recovery fits, or
who owns each buffer.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\fbox{\begin{minipage}{0.94\linewidth}
\small
\textbf{Algorithm 1: \textsc{ScheduleEvent}$(X_t,\mathcal{E}_t,\mathcal{A}_t,\pi)$}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
  \item If a needed bound was not validated under $\pi$, return \UQ and stop new optional work.
  \item Apply all events $\mathcal{E}_t$ at once: add a pending recovery for each new TB, cancel it only

  \item If the verifier accepts $X'$, commit it, send its first action, and return \QSU. Otherwise

\end{enumerate}

\noindent\textbf{Event Processing.}
Step~1 stops new optional work and returns \UQ when a needed bound was not validated under the current
conditions $\pi$. Step~2 applies all events at once: it adds a pending recovery for each new TB, cancels
it only for a timely success, and keeps it for a failure, a late result, or a missing result. Step~3
places every needed recovery and returns \MI when no all-fail schedule exists. Steps~4 and~5 visit the
waiting AI requests in a fixed order, build one candidate per request, and commit the first candidate
that the verifier accepts, which returns \QSU. Step~6 commits the recovery-only schedule and returns \QSN
when no AI request fits.

\noindent\textbf{When AI May Go First.}
For $m_h$ recoveries of length $c_h$ that must finish by the result deadline $d_h$ minus the margin
$g_h$, let $b_{\mathrm{eff}}$ include the launch overhead, the AI service time, and the completion
margin. Running AI before the recoveries is safe under the following sufficient condition:
\begin{equation}
 T_{\mathrm{dec},h}+b_{\mathrm{eff}}
 \le d_h-g_h-m_hc_h. \label{eq:window}
\end{equation}
The right side is the latest time at which the first needed recovery can start. Therefore, the
condition counts the decision and launch delay and does not assume that the AI slot starts immediately.

\noindent\textbf{Idle Time.}
The same condition shows why idle GPU time alone cannot justify admitting AI.
\begin{proposition}[Idle time is insufficient]
\label{prop:idletime}
On one GPU that runs one job at a time, with common recovery deadline $D$, pending recovery bounds
$c_1,\ldots,c_m$, and AI bound $b_{\mathrm{eff}}$, suppose that the recoveries alone fit but
\begin{equation}
  t+b_{\mathrm{eff}}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_i>D. \label{eq:idletime}
\end{equation}
Then admitting AI into idle time while ignoring pending recovery cannot guarantee the radio deadline.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Take the allowed case in which AI and every recovery take their full bounds. The last recovery then ends
after $D$ by Equation~\ref{eq:idletime}; GPU time that is idle now does not remove recovery work that may
come later.
\end{proof}

\noindent\textbf{How Much AI Fits.}
The same reasoning predicts how much AI each policy can admit. Let $W$ be the window up to the recovery
deadline, $L$ the AI deadline, $a$ one complete AI slot, $c$ one recovery bound, $M_g$ the pending
recoveries that static reservation holds, and $F_g$ the needed recoveries on recovery GPU $g$. With
$[x]_+=\max(0,x)$,
\begin{align}
 K_{\mathrm{static}}&=\sum_g\left\lfloor\frac{[\min(L,W-M_gc)]_+}{a}\right\rfloor,\\
 K_{\mathrm{RF}}&=\sum_g\left\lfloor\frac{[\min(L,W)-F_gc]_+}{a}\right\rfloor,\\
 K_{\text{\sys}}&=\sum_g\left\lfloor\frac{[\min(L,W-F_gc)]_+}{a}\right\rfloor. \label{eq:capacity}
\end{align}
Static reservation holds every pending recovery, recovery-first runs the needed recoveries before AI,
and \sys may place AI first as long as $F_gc$ of recovery time still fits before the recovery deadline.
For mixed BurstGPT request sizes, the model enumerates every subset, GPU, order, and recovery position
exactly. Failure probabilities predict the expected AI value, but the all-fail safety check does not
depend on them.

Table~\ref{tab:classes} lists the four states of a decision. They keep a scheduling limit apart from

\subsection{What \sys guarantees}
\label{sec:guarantee-scope}

\sys gives a conditional guarantee for an explicitly validated mode. Validation ties every time bound to
the setup in which it was measured and defines which events the runtime must handle.

\noindent\textbf{Mode Conditions.}
A mode fixes the GPU topology, MPS settings, process lifecycle, worker epoch, memory residency, co-running
AI, software, and the measured end-to-end time bounds. Changing any of them requires revalidation. The
180~ms period and the result deadline in our evaluation are test settings, not 5G slot or production
HARQ timing.

\noindent\textbf{Handled Events.}
The runtime handles \NRx failure and lateness, stale and duplicate messages, a lost reply before or after
a control action, a missing AI reply after the GPU finished, and reconnection of the same worker. It stops
new AI admission when completion evidence is missing.

\noindent\textbf{Excluded Events.}
Arbitrary GPU hangs, driver resets, unbounded host stalls, clock errors in production, and workloads
without validated bounds remain outside the claim. If the mandatory radio load alone does not fit, the
state is \MI; \sys does not weaken the safety check to admit it.

\subsection{Backstop implementation}
\label{sec:implementation}

\sys maps the model onto long-running CUDA workers and explicit control objects, which makes the schedule
state visible and enforceable across process and GPU boundaries.

\noindent\textbf{Radio and AI Workers.}
MIG is disabled and MPS is enabled. cuPHY instances, \NRx workers, conventional recovery, and external AI
run in separate long-running workers. TensorRT \NRx workers keep preallocated buffers and CUDA graphs,
cuPHY workers run conventional recovery, and a Qwen2.5-1.5B worker runs prefill requests of fixed sizes.
On the four-GPU node (Figure~\ref{fig:overall}b), GPU0 and GPU1 host the cuPHY instances with a 50\% MPS
share, GPU3 hosts the \NRx worker, and GPU2 hosts the cuPHY recovery worker and the Qwen worker, which
runs as a low-priority MPS client with a 20\% share. On one GPU (Figure~\ref{fig:overall}a), the same
workers share the GPU with the same MPS shares. One cuPHY receiver of our test setup holds 8.6~GiB, and
the recovery worker runs one recovery at a time. Therefore, on one GPU the recovery worker keeps one
receiver for all cells.

\noindent\textbf{Data Transfer and Control.}
CUDA IPC rings connect processes on one GPU, and NVLink rings connect GPUs. Each buffer slot carries a
request version and a completion state. The end of a copy does not mean that a receiver or a recovery
finished; the owning worker publishes a separate completion event. The coordinator stores the recovery
schedules, the buffer slots, the worker epochs, the AI launch rights, and the commit state. Unix-domain
RPC carries control messages, while GPU data stays on the IPC and NVLink paths.

\noindent\textbf{Schedule Builder and Verifier.}
\sys separates building a schedule from approving it. The fast scheduler visits the TBs and the waiting
AI requests in fixed orders and outputs a complete schedule, the ownership changes, and the mode
fingerprint; exact search serves only as a reference on small states. The independent verifier checks
the job identity, arrival, validated service time, result deadline, GPU capacity, overlap, AI slots,
buffer slot uniqueness, and version, and the runtime cannot run a candidate that the verifier rejects.
The executor runs the first action of an approved schedule, waits for its completion event, and plans
again; it never reorders GPU work from an unchecked host queue.

\noindent\textbf{Measurement.}
Our instrumentation separates decisions, host progress, GPU execution, and GPU completion, which keeps
a host timestamp from being reported as a kernel boundary. The runtime records arrival, admission,
dispatch, worker observation, CUDA completion, commit, request and payload identity, buffer version,
worker epoch, and the time a slot is freed, and Nsight traces check GPU ordering for selected modes. The
validated path uses pyAerial with long-running workers. A later native C++ path checks only input layout
and decoder output against the Python path; it is not validated for timing, and its measurements are
never mixed with the time bounds of the Python mode. Manifests keep source hashes, protocols, seeds,
excluded nodes, analyzer versions, failures, and result hashes, and every result in this paper maps to a
machine-readable file and field.

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

\subsection{Evaluation setup}
\label{sec:methodology}

\noindent\textbf{Hardware.}
We evaluate \sys on two independent nodes, each with four NVIDIA A100-SXM4-40GB GPUs connected by
third-generation NVLink. The bursty-arrival, load, and single-GPU campaigns
(Sections~\ref{sec:eval-burst} and~\ref{sec:eval-single}) run on further nodes of the same system with four
A100-SXM4-80GB GPUs each; a single-GPU run uses one of the four GPUs. MIG is disabled and MPS is enabled
on every GPU. On the four-GPU node, GPU0 and GPU1 run the two cuPHY instances with a 50\% MPS share, GPU3
runs the TensorRT \NRx with 80\%, and GPU2, the shared GPU, runs the cuPHY recovery worker with 80\% and
the Qwen worker with 20\% and low priority. We withhold the facility name for anonymous review.

\noindent\textbf{What We Measure.}
Sections~\ref{sec:eval-capacity} and~\ref{sec:eval-trace} use a capacity model checked by exact search and
BurstGPT traces, without running GPU kernels, to measure how much AI fits safely and how much of it is
served on time. Sections~\ref{sec:eval-online}-\ref{sec:eval-prediction} run the complete scheduler on
physical GPUs and measure online scheduling, AI service under bursty arrivals, the single-GPU placement
with early AI slots, whether rejected requests are really unsafe, the runtime safeguards under injected
faults, prediction accuracy, and decision cost. Section~\ref{sec:eval-sensitivity} covers the MPS setting
and the worker lifecycle, and Appendix~\ref{app:sensitivity} varies the recovery bound.

\noindent\textbf{Workloads and Test Timeline.}
Radio work uses NVIDIA Aerial cuPHY~\cite{aerial} and a TensorRT FP16 \NRx. The evaluated warm mode uses
a 180~ms period and a 155~ms result deadline (\texttt{P180/D155}). Its validated end-to-end time bounds
are $B_{\mathrm{NRx}}=45$~ms for \NRx, $B_{\mathrm{conv}}=25$~ms for a recovery, and
$B_{\mathrm{AI}}(\text{context64})=35$~ms for a 64-token AI request, with a 2~ms margin before the result
deadline. The period is not a 5G slot, and the result deadline is not a production HARQ deadline. In every
period, four admitted cells each send one single-UE, 273-PRB uplink TB; the cuPHY instance on GPU0 serves
three of them and the instance on GPU1 serves one. Qwen2.5-1.5B prefill~\cite{qwen25} with 16-512-token
prompts adds AI load on the GPU, and BurstGPT~\cite{burstgpt} supplies arrival times and prompt sizes from
four separate windows of its public trace and the prompts of the bursty campaign. Synthetic channels
drive the timing experiments; a separate Sionna CDL-D/E mode tests the radio value of \NRx
(Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\noindent\textbf{Baselines.}
Every baseline makes the same radio decisions and sees the same arrivals, bounds, and workers as \sys.
Two baselines follow the sharing approaches of Section~\ref{sec:related}. \emph{Static reservation}
holds the recovery of every admitted TB until the result deadline, as reservation-based sharing does, and
\emph{idle-time admission} admits AI into GPU time that is idle when it decides and ignores pending
recovery, as sharing that uses any idle GPU time does. The \emph{recovery-first} policy is an ablation of
\sys: it keeps pending-recovery tracking, the all-fail admission check, and one commit per TB, but it runs
every needed recovery before AI, which isolates the effect of putting AI first. The \emph{best possible}
schedule sees the whole trace in advance and bounds the value that any policy can reach. Idle-time
admission and uncontrolled MPS serve only to expose wrong assumptions; they are not safe alternatives.

\noindent\textbf{How We Run Experiments.}
Before looking at results, each main campaign fixes its source code, workload, bounds, seeds, nodes,
excluded nodes, analyzer, and stopping rule. Development and held-out runs use different seeds and nodes,
and failed, negative, and \UQ runs stay in the record. We report zero observed events as results on a
finite sample, not as probabilities or worst-case execution time (WCET) guarantees.

\paragraph{Reproducibility.}
The anonymous artifact package contains the source code, protocols, manifests, analyzers, exact checker,
independent verifier, result summaries, and figure scripts. The GPU campaigns need proprietary
Aerial/cuPHY and TensorRT components; the model, checker, trace, and analysis do not. Node names and
absolute paths are removed from the review artifact.

\paragraph{Use of generative AI.}
Generative-AI tools helped with language editing, organization, bibliography cleanup, and
consistency-checking scripts. The authors designed the system and model, fixed the protocols, ran the
experiments, inspected failures, and checked every claim against hashed artifacts. Generative AI produced
no experimental measurement or ground-truth label.

the best schedule places no AI request. With 100, 250, and 1,000~ms, recovery-first reaches 75.8\%,
98.0\%, and 100\% of the best on average. The screen keeps the 362 settings at which recovery-first falls
at least 5\% behind the best in every window: 298 at a 100~ms SLO and 64 at 250~ms.

% provenance: C175_LEVERS
\noindent\textbf{Value at These Settings.}
Figure~\ref{fig:eval-trace}a shows that \sys closes this gap. On one GPU, recovery-first delivers 51.7\%
of the best fixed placement and \sys delivers 99.8\%; on two and four GPUs, recovery-first delivers
67.4\% and 75.5\%, while \sys stays above 99.8\%. Figure~\ref{fig:eval-trace}b shows that \sys delivers
1.44$\times$ the value of recovery-first at the median and up to 17.2$\times$. The AI-first greedy order
of \sys is within 5\% of the best fixed placement at 361 of the 362 settings, and the remaining setting
has a 7.02\% gap. \sys gets there by placing AI before the needed recoveries whenever the all-fail
schedule still ends before the recovery deadline, while recovery-first puts every AI request behind the
recoveries and misses tight AI SLOs. Moving recoveries flexibly across GPUs would help at 203 settings in
an upper-bound model: 0/102 with one GPU, 145/175 with two, and 58/85 with four, with a 10.64\% median
gain. Because this model ignores the cost of moving work and was not validated on GPUs, it is a design
target, and we make no executed optimizer claim. Choosing among the fixed Sionna channel settings to
reduce pending recoveries, with no or 0.02 loss of radio value, removes no pending recovery.

% provenance: C159_BASELINE
\noindent\textbf{Original Trace.}
The gain depends on how much room the workload leaves. In the original physical mode, a calibration-only
exact screen gives static reservation, recovery-first, \sys, and the best schedule the same radio
decisions, arrivals, bounds, and one Qwen request per period. After we fixed an error in how absolute
deadlines were read, without dropping any request or opening the held-out data, exact matching assigns
856 timely requests and 349,387 tokens to recovery-first, \sys, and the best schedule, and static
reservation leaves no validated Qwen slot in the same period. Charging every needed recovery its full
bound leaves recovery-first at 345,405 tokens, only 1.14\% below \sys and below the 5\% threshold set in
advance. Thus, the original trace leaves no room for ordering, and recovery-first stays the strong safe
baseline for it.

\label{sec:eval-online}

\noindent\textbf{Two-Node Campaign.}

\subsection{AI service under bursty arrivals}
\label{sec:eval-burst}

% provenance: C176_BURST
\noindent\textbf{Bursty Workload.}
AI requests arrive in bursts, and during a burst the scheduler decides how much AI each recovery window
can take. Therefore, we replay one sequence of 329 BurstGPT prompts on the physical four-GPU path at 3
requests/s for 108~s. The prompts are drawn from all BurstGPT requests and rounded up to the six prompt
lengths, and 47.4\% of them round to 512 tokens. Six arrival patterns use this sequence and differ only in
arrival times: evenly spaced arrivals, the per-second arrivals of a BurstGPT segment, and Gamma arrivals
whose gaps have a coefficient of variation (CV) of 1 (Poisson), 2, 4, and 8. Every request must receive
its first token within 200~ms, and up to four AI slots share one period in arrival order. All policies see
the same \NRx results for a pattern, which makes each comparison paired.

\noindent\textbf{AI Service.}
Figure~\ref{fig:eval-burst}a shows the AI tokens served within their SLO. \sys serves 6.2-18.5\% more

admits requests whose recoveries no longer fit before the recovery deadline. As the arrivals become
burstier, every policy serves fewer tokens because a burst is larger than one period can hold and its
requests expire.

\noindent\textbf{Radio Safety under Bursts.}
Figure~\ref{fig:eval-burst}b shows the price of that extra AI. Idle-time admission breaks the recovery

result deadline. \sys and recovery-first never miss the recovery deadline, and no radio result misses the
result deadline in any of their 18 runs. In the same padded runs, \sys serves 10.6-18.5\% more on-time
tokens than recovery-first.

\noindent\textbf{Throughput under Load.}
Figure~\ref{fig:eval-load} raises the AI load with Poisson arrivals. With a 200~ms SLO, \sys serves
10.6-21.2\% more on-time AI tokens than recovery-first from 1.92 to 4.95 requests/s, and the two nearly
tie at 0.98 requests/s. Static reservation serves no AI request at any load, and idle-time admission
serves the most but breaks the recovery deadline at the declared bounds in 19-88 of 600 periods. With a
1,000~ms SLO (Figure~\ref{fig:eval-load}b), \sys and recovery-first reach the same throughput at every
load because both fit the same AI work and needed recoveries into each window. Thus, tracking pending
recovery decides how much AI one window can hold, and putting AI first decides how much of it meets a
tight deadline.

\noindent\textbf{Where the Gain Comes From.}
Figure~\ref{fig:eval-burst}c varies the AI SLO in a replay that uses the measured service times of the
two-node campaign, averaged over five seeds. The gain of \sys over recovery-first shrinks as the SLO
loosens: 34.3\% on average at 150~ms, 14.2\% at 200~ms, 5.3\% at 300~ms, and 2.7\% at 500~ms. The measured
gains at 200~ms, 6.2-18.5\%, lie close to the replay range of 8.8-21.4\%. \sys gets this gain because it
places a checked AI slot ahead of the needed recoveries, while recovery-first must first give every needed
recovery its full bound before an AI slot can meet its SLO. Thus, the order matters for interactive AI
SLOs and fades for loose ones, which agrees with the tie on the original trace in
Section~\ref{sec:eval-trace}.

Figure~\ref{fig:eval-single}a pairs the 30 natural runs on one GPU with their four-GPU runs. The on-time AI

recovery deadline, and the run stops. In addition, 5 of the 42 single-GPU runs lose an \NRx result at the
first \NRx deadline after startup. In two of them, the owners stall for up to 0.35~s, the first period's
recoveries miss the deadline, 8 radio results miss the result deadline, and the run stops. None of the 39
four-GPU runs shows any of these events. Thus, the single-GPU placement is a separate mode: it needs a
warm-up before the first TB and time bounds measured on one GPU, and we report its runs as development
evidence.

On one GPU, an early AI slot shares the GPU with \NRx (Section~\ref{sec:design-recovery}). We pair \sys

all-fail schedule already holds the recovery of every admitted TB when the TBs arrive. The planner skips
slots that cannot end in time before it calls the verifier, which keeps planning within the 5~ms launch
budget when a burst fills the queue.

\subsection{Runtime safeguards and faults}
\label{sec:eval-refinement}

\noindent\textbf{Latest Start Time.}
A committed schedule stays valid only if the GPU cannot start a slot later than the schedule assumes.
Figure~\ref{fig:eval-refinement}a shows when each slot reaches the Qwen worker, relative to its latest
start time. All 1,077 slots of the two-node campaign arrive 2.9-3.8~ms before the latest start time and
launch. A fault that holds the AI slot delays 52 slots until 6.4-7.2~ms after the latest start time, and
the worker refuses all 52 before launch with a ``not launched'' reply. Without this check, these requests
would run up to 7.2~ms later than their schedules allow and push the needed recoveries toward the recovery
deadline. \sys prevents this by attaching the latest start time to the slot at commit and checking it in
the worker right before kernel submission.

\noindent\textbf{Radio Commit Time.}
Putting AI first delays the needed recoveries, and Figure~\ref{fig:eval-refinement}b measures this cost on
every TB of the two-node campaign. The 3,488 TBs with a timely \NRx success commit at the \NRx deadline,
at a median of 46.1~ms after arrival, whatever the AI slot. Recovered TBs commit at a median of 62.1~ms
when no AI slot runs before the recovery, 72.7-73.7~ms after a 16-128-token slot, 86.0~ms after a
256-token slot, and 116.4~ms after a 512-token slot. The latest commit, 118.022~ms, is 37.0~ms before the

that follow it.

% provenance: C160_FAULT
\noindent\textbf{Fault Handling.}
A stale message or a lost reply can free the wrong buffer slot even when the schedule is correct.
Therefore, we combine model checking with fault injection on GPUs. A model checker explores 51
combinations of worker epoch, version, completion, and marker state and finds no invariant violation. The
fault campaign injects seven kinds of faults on the two validated nodes. Figure~\ref{fig:eval-refinement}c
shows that every TB of every kind commits exactly once before the result deadline. The largest commit time
is 125.2~ms, when every TB of a period fails and all four recoveries run, and it stays 29.8~ms before the
155~ms result deadline. The campaign runs 700 periods, 2,800 \NRx requests, 942 recoveries, and 2,800
commits without an observed deadline miss, including 40 replayed messages, four fatal faults, and 260
periods after them; 0/2,800 misses gives a 95\% upper bound of 0.107\%. On one additional node, the first
\NRx result exceeded its bound, and we keep that run as \UQ without pooling it. Thus, fault handling holds
on the two validated nodes for the declared fault and lifecycle kinds.

\subsection{MPS setting and worker lifecycle}
\label{sec:eval-sensitivity}

% provenance: Q1_MPS_DIAGNOSTICS
\noindent\textbf{MPS Setting.}
MPS controls how processes share the GPU but does not bound time. When an AI client overruns, the radio
misses 2 of 1,500 deadlines with a 100\% MPS cap and 1 of 1,500 with a 20\% cap. In a matched lifecycle
test, a CPU-only control misses 0 of 10,000 deadlines and a GPU client under MPS misses 12 of 10,000. The
two tests ran separately and do not show that a smaller cap is always safer. Thus, neither the MPS cap nor
the current queue can decide admission.

% provenance: C164_LIFECYCLE
\noindent\textbf{Worker Lifecycle.}
Restarting or idling a worker changes the service times that a schedule relies on. The lifecycle matrix
contains five validated or partly validated subsets and five \UQ modes. The validated evidence covers warm
long-running workers, the first request after 30~s idle, an MPS restart followed by revalidation, radio
continuity while Qwen reloads, and reconnecting the same worker. Whether AI stays available during a
restart or reload is outside the claim. Cold starts of a process or a model, 5~min and 30~min idle, garbage
collection enabled, and replacing a worker process remain \UQ. A single-node replacement result is
``Development canary only'' because it lacks independent held-out runs.
Durable journal recovery would be a new mechanism and remains outside this paper.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\noindent\textbf{GPU Sharing in AI-RAN and vRAN.}
Earlier work on AI-RAN and virtualized RAN (vRAN) platforms shares compute between deadline-critical RAN
functions and other workloads. YinYangRAN runs 5G PHY and ML workloads together on GPU
accelerators~\cite{yinyangran}, CloudRIC shares pools of different accelerators among distributed
units~\cite{cloudric}, and Concordia reuses idle vRAN CPU capacity while meeting processing
deadlines~\cite{concordia}. Other work places AI and RAN workloads through MIG partitions, mixed hardware,
and worker location~\cite{interplay,caora,haf,distributedairan}, while ARCHES switches PHY models, OCUDU
exposes timing classes inside the DU, and SMEC coordinates RAN and edge SLOs~\cite{arches,ocudu,smec}.

Our work shares the goal of reusing RAN compute for other workloads. However, these systems schedule
resources or queued jobs, and their public designs do not represent the recovery that a neural receiver
may need. \sys fills this gap by tracking each pending recovery and by combining one all-fail recovery
schedule across cuPHY instances with an AI slot that stays owned until the GPU finishes.

\noindent\textbf{Real-Time GPU Scheduling and Backup Reservation.}
Earlier work on GPU scheduling makes the latency of concurrent GPU work predictable through
microsecond-scale preemption~\cite{reef}, preemptive scheduling across accelerators~\cite{xsched}, space
and time sharing for real-time DNN inference~\cite{daris}, and hardware partitioning of GPU
compute~\cite{nvtaskset}. Fault-tolerant real-time scheduling reserves a backup copy for each primary task,
frees the backup when the primary succeeds, and overlaps the backups of primaries that cannot fail
together~\cite{ghosh1997primarybackup}. Imprecise computation splits a task into mandatory and optional
parts and runs the optional part only when time remains~\cite{liu1991imprecise}.

Our work builds on these reservation and preemption techniques. However, preemption and partitioning
control kernels that are already queued, and overlapping backups assumes that primaries do not fail
together. \sys fills this gap by checking one all-fail schedule for the recovery of every pending
receiver, releasing a recovery when its receiver succeeds in time, and enforcing that schedule through GPU
launch, buffer ownership, and GPU completion.

\noindent\textbf{Scope of This Comparison.}
Across the public designs we reviewed through September 2026, we found no system that combines an
executable all-fail schedule for the pending recovery of each TB, AI slots held until the GPU finishes,
and a prediction of admissible states that uses only validated bounds. This is a finding about the
literature we reviewed, not a claim about unpublished work or work that uses other terms.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\noindent\textbf{Claim Boundary.}
The guarantee holds only under the validated bounds and measurement conditions of the evaluated mode.
\sys does not claim that MPS gives hard isolation, that measured maxima are WCETs, or that our test
timeline is a production HARQ deadline. Five lifecycle subsets are validated or partly validated and five
remain \UQ; the claim does not require or imply a 10/10 lifecycle matrix.

\noindent\textbf{Test Timeline.}
\texttt{P180/D155} is a test timeline: $P$ is the time between TB arrivals, not a 5G slot length, and
\texttt{D155} is not a production HARQ deadline. A builder and checker for production \texttt{d\_MAC}
traces pass 24 unit tests, but no trace from a target DU/FAPI stack is available. A new timeline requires
revalidation on GPUs; we make no production HARQ guarantee.

% provenance: P2_FAST_PATH_AND_STAGE
\noindent\textbf{Production Timing.}
Aerial testMAC expects an uplink indication with the CRC result by $T0+4.5$~ms. Without AI and with warm
workers, three simple paths, which wait for \NRx and then recover, decode speculatively on the same GPU,
or precompute across GPUs, finish 0/1,000 within 4.5~ms. Moving channel estimation through CRC to GPU1 with
raw-IQ P2P reaches 852/1,000, and a shared cuPHY/TensorRT stream reaches 885/1,000. The fixed long-running,
stream-ordered fast path reaches 995/1,000 with a 3.813~ms median and a 3.995~ms p99, but five late
completions fail the threshold, and the held-out runs stay closed. A 300-request profile traces the
remaining tail to remote channel estimation: its GPU time has a p50/p99/max of 0.866/4.873/9.592~ms, and
its correlation with the end-to-end pair time is 0.9603. P2P copies average 36.261/17.150~$\mu$s, derate
matching exposes a 1,060.105~$\mu$s host call despite a 0.168~ms GPU p99, TensorRT enqueue averages
7.443~$\mu$s, and the TensorRT graph has a 0.886~ms GPU p99. These profiles are mechanism evidence rather
than validation or WCET. TestMAC is a controlled developer L2, not a source of field-DU $d_{\mathrm{MAC}}$
timing.

% provenance: P3_CHANNEL_HOLDOUT
\noindent\textbf{Channel Scope.}
External Aerial TDL-A remains unsupported: across raw and normalized inputs, public reference radio
settings, one-antenna channel execution in frequency and time, and an FP32 engine built like the public
notebook, conventional decoding succeeds in every ten-TB test and \NRx succeeds in none. A fixed
development grid selects Sionna CDL-D/E at 100~ns and keeps the failures of CDL-A/B/C, and held-out runs
with separate seeds use five Es/No levels and 50 blocks per level and model. Both receivers pass 50/50 at
10~dB. In CDL-D, the conventional receiver decodes 153/250 blocks and \NRx 164/250, with 16 blocks decoded
only by \NRx and five only by the conventional receiver; in CDL-E, the counts are 155/250 and 163/250, with
15 and seven. The 31 neural-only versus 12 conventional-only outcomes give a two-sided exact $p=0.00540$:
optional \NRx has conditional radio value in the fixed Sionna CDL-D/E mode. That channel has not passed
production timing or end-to-end validation.

\noindent\textbf{Finite Samples.}
Zero observed violations prove neither a WCET nor a zero failure probability. Bounds belong to one
combination of hardware, software, placement, workload, and lifecycle. All main hardware results use A100
GPUs, and the NVLink results do not carry over to other GPU families or to data paths between nodes.

% provenance: C158_LONG_TAIL
\noindent\textbf{Unresolved Long Tail.}
In one development attempt, one \NRx completion took 350.948\,ms while the p99 of the 400-request run was
8.111\,ms; the run had 4/400 deadline misses. Targeted campaigns with idle periods, MPS restarts with
revalidation, Qwen reloads, and reconnects did not reproduce it. Its cause is unknown, and GPU and driver
hangs remain outside the fault model.

\noindent\textbf{Workload Mapping.}
BurstGPT supplies real arrival times and prompt sizes, and Qwen2.5-1.5B prefill stands in for external AI
work. The prompt-size classes and AI deadlines are our experimental choices; other workloads need new
bounds and a new validation.

\section{Conclusion}

In this paper, we present \sys, a recovery-safe GPU scheduler that shares AI-RAN GPUs between neural
receivers and AI inference. By tracking each pending recovery and admitting AI only when an all-fail
schedule still fits, \sys keeps radio recovery within its deadline while it reuses the GPU time that
neural-receiver successes free. Our evaluation shows that \sys reaches the best possible AI capacity at
13,716 feasible model settings and delivers a median of 1.44$\times$ the timely AI value of recovery-first
at the trace settings where order matters. On two four-GPU nodes, \sys has 0/120 safety violations in
boundary tests where idle-time admission fails 40/40, serves 6.2-18.5\% more on-time AI tokens than
recovery-first under bursty AI arrivals, and commits every TB exactly once under seven kinds of injected
faults. On one GPU, early AI slots add 2.6-70.3\% more on-time AI tokens without losing a timely \NRx
result. These results validate a test timeline, not production HARQ timing.
